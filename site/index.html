Recovery Phrase Backup

Back up the recovery phrase of your Ethereum, Bitcoin or other crypto wallet so that no single place can lose it, or reveal it.

Split your 12 to 24 words (also called a seed phrase) into shares. Any two of three (or the numbers you choose) give your phrase back; one share alone reveals nothing. Free, open source, and it works with every wallet.

Who it's for

  • Your only copy is a sheet of paper. It sits in a drawer or a safe, where one fire, flood or tidy-up loses it for good. Split it into three shares, at home, in a bank box and with a friend, any two of which recover it: losing one loses nothing.
  • Your phrase is stamped on steel. The plate that came with your Ledger or Trezor survives a fire, but a burglar who finds it has your wallet. Keep the device as it is, and replace the plate with shares in four places, any three of which recover the phrase: a thief would have to find three.
  • You want your family to inherit it. Today nobody else can reach your funds, and if something happened to you, nobody ever could. Make two groups of shares, three for your family and three for trusted friends, with any two from each group needed, and leave the "how to recover" sheet with your will. Together they can recover it without you; that's the point. Neither group can do it alone, and each can lose a share.
  • The wallet belongs to an organisation. A company or association shouldn't depend on one person. With 3 of 5 board members, nobody can recover it alone, and it survives two people losing their share. This backs up the phrase; it isn't multisig, so whoever recovers it holds the whole wallet.
  • Someone holding a share is no longer the right person. A friend moves away, or you fall out. Make a new backup with new keepers, then destroy the old shares: until they're gone, the old backup still recovers your phrase.
  • You plan to hold for twenty years. Will this tool still exist then? It doesn't need to. The shares follow an open standard that other tools also recover, and the tool itself is one file you keep next to them.

How it works

  1. Type your phrase. The page shows its fingerprint and first Ethereum and Bitcoin addresses, so you can check it's your wallet. Test phrases get a warning.
  2. Choose how to split it: 2 of 3, 3 of 5, or your own groups, such as "2 of 3 places, each needing 2 of 3 people".
  3. Record each share by hand or on printed cards, and type back three of its words to check your copy.
  4. Test the recovery from your written shares before you rely on them.
  5. Give the shares to people and places you trust. Each card asks its keeper to keep it safe and return it only to you, and carries nothing a finder could use: no address, no share number, not even how many shares exist.
  6. Leave a "how to recover" sheet for your heir. It holds no secret, only what to do and who has the shares.

Recovery gives back your original words, so you restore them in the same wallet as always: MetaMask, Ledger, Trezor or any other wallet that gave you 12 to 24 words (BIP39). No extra hardware needed. The shares use SLIP-39, an open standard: they also recover with other SLIP-39 tools, which this project checks on every change.

You don't have to trust me

You shouldn't have to trust anyone with your recovery phrase, including me, the author. The tool is built so that you don't:

  • It can't send anything. You run it on a live USB system such as Tails with the network off, so even a broken or malicious version has no way out.
  • You can check what you run. The source is public and small enough to read, and the build is reproducible: anyone can rebuild the file and get exactly the same bytes as the download.
  • Few dependencies. No JavaScript libraries and nothing loaded from elsewhere. The cryptography comes from a few widely used open-source Rust libraries.
  • No new cryptography. It uses two open standards, BIP39 for your words and SLIP-39 for the shares.
  • You don't depend on this project. Shares are tested against other SLIP-39 tools on every change, so you can recover without this one.
  • Keep a copy of the tool. It's a single web page, with nothing to install and no server behind it, and web pages are made to keep working: browsers still open pages from the 1990s, because breaking old pages is the one thing they avoid. Store the file with your shares or your "how to recover" sheet. If it ever stopped opening, any other SLIP-39 tool can still recover your shares.

What's left to trust: that the shares are made with good randomness. A recovery test can't show this; only reading the code can. That's why the code is kept small, and why version 1 waits for an independent review.

"Never type your phrase into a website"

That's the right rule, and this tool keeps it. The demo on this site accepts only its own published sample phrases. For your real phrase, you download the tool: a single file that you open in your browser with the network switched off. It is blocked from making any connection, saves nothing, and forgets everything when you close it.

Offline alone isn't enough if the computer itself can't be trusted: malware or a malicious browser extension can record what you type and send it the next time you go online. That's why the recommended way is to start the computer from a live USB system such as Tails: a clean system that doesn't touch your usual one and forgets everything when you shut it down.

  1. Download recovery-phrase-backup.html (version {{VERSION}}). Its SHA-256 is {{RELEASE_SHA256}}.
  2. Check the hash (shasum -a 256 recovery-phrase-backup.html). To check that the file matches the source, rebuild it from the latest commit with Docker (scripts/build-reproducible.sh) and compare.
  3. Start the computer from a live USB system such as Tails, and don't connect it to the network. If you can't, at least disconnect, and use a fresh browser profile with no extensions.
  4. In Tor Browser (the browser on Tails), set the security level to Standard: Safer and Safest block WebAssembly, which the tool needs. For this one file, offline, Standard is fine.
  5. Open the file and follow the steps.

Before you rely on it

  • Use it on a clean system, offline. The page sends nothing, but other software on the computer could, such as a malicious extension, even later once you're back online. A live USB system rules that out.
  • Printing puts everything in one place until you cut the cards apart. Use a printer at home, never "Save as PDF", or write the shares by hand.
  • Your wallet's own passphrase (the "25th word"), if it has one, is not part of the backup. Back it up separately.
  • Reviewed, not audited. The code is reviewed by several AI models before release, and what they find is fixed. That is a second pair of eyes, not a professional audit.

Recovery without this tool

Your backup doesn't depend on this project still existing. Any SLIP-39 tool, such as Trezor's reference implementation (shamir recover), recovers the secret from your shares as hex, and any BIP39 tool turns that hex back into your words.
